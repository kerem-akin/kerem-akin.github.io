Shards of Balance — a 2-D action platformer, built solo

GameMaker Language Self-taught Solo project 6 levels + boss fight

A Python course taught me I loved coding; what I wanted to build was a world. So I taught myself GameMaker Language from scratch and spent months on Shards of Balance: a pixel-art platformer where the Crystal of Balance has shattered and you fight through six levels of a corrupted realm to restore it. Every system in it is mine — built while I was preparing for my GCSE exams.

The title screen — new game, continue, or quit.

What's in itSix hand-built levels; five enemy types with distinct behaviour — chasing skeletons, tanky plants, ranged fire spirits, speed-draining skulls and archers — plus an enemy spawner; three story NPCs; potions, breakable crates and fire traps; and a final boss with three attack phases.

Level one, and the hidden cavern biome with its own lighting and particle systems.

The problem I'm proudest ofDifficulty had to rise without a new enemy for every level. So I built variants: one enemy object reads its speed, damage and health from its creation event and spawns an effect above itself — a blue aura for fast versions, red for high-damage ones — so the player reads the threat instantly. One object, many difficulty tiers, and the game teaches its own rules without a tutorial.
Built from nothingDialogue, camera control, room transitions, screen shake, parallax backgrounds, particles, a white-flash damage shader, a save system, and a cutscene engine.

The Witch's Infernal Blaze recharges every 30 wand shots — and the final fight against Exilion.

What I'd fixWriting my own evaluation was the most useful part of the project. Honest verdict: the enemy AI is reactive rather than smart, the menu is plain, and the jump in difficulty before the boss is uneven. The next version needs varied enemy behaviour, a second weapon, and a rebalanced early game.

The player's guide I designed, and hand-drawn art from the closing cutscene.

TSA Engineering Design — Modular Smartphone Microscope

Optics & CAD (Onshape) 3D printing 13 design iterations TSA Competition 2026

Many classrooms lack working microscopes — yet nearly every student carries a high-resolution camera. For the TSA theme "Engineering the Tools of Scientific Discovery," our team designed a MagSafe-mounted, 3D-printed modular microscope that turns a smartphone into a functional lab instrument, designed to cost under $25.

How we startedWe bought a real microscope and took it apart to understand its optics, then designed our own: a rotating 4×/10×/40× lens carousel with magnetic click-stops, a screw-lift focus stage, and a modular LED illumination box.

The first hand sketches — where every iteration started.

The problem I like bestMagSafe rings are perfect circles, so the mount rotated slightly on every attach and threw the lens out of alignment with the camera. We added an angular jut-out that registers against the phone's camera bump — turning alignment from a user-skill problem into a mechanical guarantee.
Iteration, including failuresThirteen documented iterations: a clip-style mount that couldn't centre (eliminated), a drop-in lens carousel where lenses shifted (redesigned with threaded seats), a rack-and-pinion focus system too bulky for a phone (replaced by a screw-lift), a light box too big to hold comfortably (shrunk and made modular). Each failure drove the next design.

Three printed versions of one part — the focus knob.

ResultThe prototype resolves onion epidermal cells at 4×, 10× and 40× — these are photographs taken through it with a phone. My role: CAD co-lead on the carousel, arm and stage; the dissection; the sketches; and the engineering portfolio.

Onion epidermis at 4×, 10× and 40× — wide field, defined cell walls, then fine structure at the cost of brightness and depth of field.

One habit across three projectsA game, a website and a microscope don't look related — but each came down to the same loop: build the smallest version, break it on purpose, find the real cause, rebuild.

Operation Catapult — Rose-Hulman Institute of Technology

Summer engineering program Team build Hands-on prototyping

The assignment at Rose-Hulman's Operation Catapult was precision, not power: build a catapult that lands golf balls at 10, 20 and 30 feet on demand. Working as a two-person team, we went from research and projectile-motion calculations to a launch-tested machine in six days.

Prototype 1 → 3Our first build launched, but the same pull produced different distances — the release angle wasn't repeatable. We switched to a spring-loaded A-frame, which made distance a function of three controllable variables (mass, spring constant, pull distance) at a fixed launch angle. The third prototype restructured the frame entirely, adding mounting points to kill the flex that was still costing us accuracy.
My roleGroup lead of the two-person team; we designed, built and tested every part together.
